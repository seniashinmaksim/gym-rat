# Hypertrophy Evidence Brief (verified)

Compiled 2026-11-21. Every reference below was checked for title, authors, journal and year against Europe PMC/PubMed or Crossref, and its DOI was confirmed to resolve at doi.org. Findings are paraphrased from abstracts or open full texts. Where a number comes only from a preprint version, or a full text could not be read, this brief says so. "SMD" means standardized mean difference and "RIR" means reps in reserve.

## 1. The four user-supplied references

### 1.1 Pelland et al. — weekly volume and frequency dose-response
- **Citation:** Pelland JC, Remmert JF, Robinson ZP, Hinson SR, Zourdos MC. *The Resistance Training Dose Response: Meta-Regressions Exploring the Effects of Weekly Volume and Frequency on Muscle Hypertrophy and Strength Gains.* Sports Med 2026;56(2):481–505 (online 4 Dec 2025). DOI 10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w. PMID 41343037. **Verified.** The DOI is correct, but the year is 2026 in print (2025 online).
- **Design:** Bayesian multilevel meta-regressions of 67 studies and 2,058 participants. Before analysis, the authors labeled every set "direct" or "indirect" for the measured muscle. They then counted indirect sets three ways: 1 ("total"), 0.5 ("fractional") or 0 ("direct"). Models were adjusted for duration and training status. The full text is paywalled. The details below come from the open SportRxiv preprint (DOI 10.51224/SRXIV.460, Sept 2024 version), and the published abstract is consistent with them.
- **Set counting:** For both volume and frequency, and for both outcomes, the fractional method had the strongest support. Bayes-factor evidence was "strong" to "very strong" over both direct-only and total counting. The authors read this as indirect sets giving roughly half the stimulus of a direct set. They note that a flat 0.5 for every indirect set is itself a simplification.
- **Shape of the volume curve (hypertrophy):** Gains rose with volume, with a 100% posterior probability that the slope was positive. The best-fitting curve was a square root, which means diminishing returns but no inverted U. The preprint's "efficiency tiers" are:
  - about 4 fractional sets per week gave detectable growth;
  - 5–10 sets was the most efficient range;
  - 11–18 sets needed about 8.5 extra sets to produce a further detectable gain;
  - 19–29 sets needed about 11 extra sets;
  - 30–42 sets needed about 12.5 extra sets;
  - above about 43 sets, data were too sparse to say.

  The fixed effects explained only about 22% of the variance. The tier cut-points may differ slightly in the published version (unverified).
- **Strength:** Diminishing returns were much stronger for strength, which reached a functional plateau after a few sets per week.
- **Frequency:** At equal volume, frequency had a negligible and inconsistent effect on hypertrophy (posterior probability of a positive slope 91.3%, with the interval including zero). For strength, frequency had a clear positive effect with diminishing returns: about +3.3% strength gain per extra weekly session at the mean frequency.
- **Per-session ceiling:** This paper did not model per-session volume. A companion preprint did: Remmert et al. 2025, *Is There Too Much of a Good Thing? Meta-Regressions of the Effect of Per-Session Volume on Hypertrophy and Strength*, SportRxiv, DOI 10.51224/SRXIV.537 (verified preprint, **not peer reviewed**; no journal version found). It found that per-session volume helped both outcomes, with diminishing returns. Its "point of undetectable outcome superiority" was about 11 fractional sets per session for hypertrophy (about 8 direct sets) and about 2 direct sets for strength. Data at very high per-session volumes were sparse, so the authors could not say whether more sets eventually hurt.
- **Quality:** These are large, preregistered-style Bayesian meta-regressions. However, the analyses are observational across studies rather than randomized comparisons, explained variance is modest, and the direct/indirect labels were judgment calls.

### 1.2 ACSM Position Stand (2026)
- **Citation:** Currier BS, D'Souza AC, Fiatarone Singh MA, Lowisz CV, Rawson ES, Schoenfeld BJ, Smith-Ryan AE, Steen JP, Thomas GA, Triplett NT, Washington TA, Werner TJ, Phillips SM. *American College of Sports Medicine Position Stand. Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults: An Overview of Reviews.* Med Sci Sports Exerc 2026;58(4):851–872. DOI 10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897. PMID 41843416, PMC12965823 (open access). **Verified.**
- **Design:** An umbrella review of 137 systematic reviews covering more than 30,000 adults, with searches through October 2024. It is the highest level of synthesis available, but it inherits the low quality of many underlying trials.
- **Hypertrophy findings:**
  - **Volume:** Higher volume (at least 10 sets per week) and eccentric overload enhanced hypertrophy, with a dose-response. The discussion cites diminishing returns at roughly 18–20 weekly sets.
  - **Load:** Load did not change hypertrophy across roughly 30–100% of 1RM.
  - **Proximity to failure:** Training to failure did not consistently improve hypertrophy. The authors suggest about 2–3 RIR as a sufficient stimulus, while saying evidence cannot pin down exact RIR targets.
  - **Frequency:** Frequency did not matter when volume was equated. The general advice is at least 2 sessions per week.
  - **Rest intervals:** Effect could not be determined.
  - **Range of motion:** Effect could not be determined for hypertrophy. Full ROM favored strength.
  - **Other variables:** Periodization and exercise order did not affect hypertrophy.
  - **Progression:** Progressive overload is not strictly required short term but is probably needed for long-term progress.
  - **Strength (for contrast):** Heavier loads, full ROM and training at least twice weekly improved strength.

### 1.3 Robinson et al. (2024) — proximity to failure
- **Citation:** Robinson ZP, Pelland JC, Remmert JF, Refalo MC, Jukic I, Steele J, Zourdos MC. *Exploring the Dose-Response Relationship Between Estimated Resistance Training Proximity to Failure, Strength Gain, and Muscle Hypertrophy: A Series of Meta-Regressions.* Sports Med 2024;54(9):2209–2231. DOI 10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2. PMID 38970765. **Verified.** The published title adds the subtitle "A Series of Meta-Regressions".
- **Design:** Multilevel meta-regressions in which RIR was **estimated** from study descriptions, because almost no studies reported it. Models were adjusted for load, how volume was equated, duration and training status. The preprint analyzed 26 studies (139 effects) for hypertrophy and 55 studies for strength. Published counts are unverified.
- **Findings:**
  - **Hypertrophy:** Gains increased as sets ended closer to failure. The relationship was non-linear: the best fit was a linear-log curve, so the steepest changes occur near 0 RIR and the curve flattens at higher RIR.
  - **Strength:** Gains were similar across a wide RIR range.
- **Quality:** The authors call the analysis exploratory. Model fit was modest, and estimating RIR is the main limitation. The model does not say where the curve reaches zero, but sets far from failure still showed some growth.

### 1.4 Refalo et al. (2023) — failure vs non-failure
- **Citation:** Refalo MC, Helms ER, Trexler ET, Hamilton DL, Fyfe JJ. *Influence of Resistance Training Proximity-to-Failure on Skeletal Muscle Hypertrophy: A Systematic Review with Meta-analysis.* Sports Med 2023;53(3):649–665. DOI 10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y. PMID 36334240, PMC9935748 (open access). **Verified.**
- **Design:** Systematic review with meta-analysis of 15 studies in healthy adults.
- **Findings:**
  - **Set failure vs non-failure:** A trivial advantage for set failure (ES 0.19, 95% CI 0.00–0.37).
  - **Momentary failure vs non-failure:** No significant difference (ES 0.12).
  - **Higher vs moderate velocity-loss thresholds:** No difference.
  - **Conclusion:** No evidence that training to momentary failure is superior. The relationship may be non-linear: getting reasonably close matters, but reaching absolute failure adds little.
- **Supporting RCT:** Refalo et al. 2024, J Sports Sci 42(1):85–101, DOI 10.1080/02640414.2024.2321021 (verified). In trained lifters, each leg trained either to failure or to about 1–2 RIR, and quadriceps growth was virtually identical.

## 2. Additional evidence

### 2.1 Load / rep range
- **Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn & Krieger 2017** (J Strength Cond Res 31(12):3508–3523; DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200; verified). Meta-analysis of 21 studies, all sets to failure, comparing loads at or below 60% 1RM with loads above 60%. Hypertrophy was similar; 1RM strength favored heavy loads.
- **Lopez et al. 2021** (Med Sci Sports Exerc 53(6):1206–1216; DOI 10.1249/MSS.0000000000002585; verified). Network meta-analysis of 28 studies and 747 adults, all to failure. Hypertrophy did not differ between low (>15RM), moderate (9–15RM) and high (≤8RM) loads. Strength favored high and moderate loads over low loads.
- **Schoenfeld, Contreras, Vigotsky & Peterson 2016** (J Sports Sci Med 15(4):715–722; PMID 27928218, PMC5131226). **No DOI is registered for this paper**; it was verified by PMID and PMC instead. Trained men did equal sets of 2–4RM or 8–12RM. Heavy loading gave more squat strength, and moderate loading gave more thigh growth.
- **Answer:** When sets are taken close to failure, heavier loads do **not** produce more hypertrophy than moderate or light loads; growth is similar from roughly 30% to over 80% 1RM. Heavy loads **do** produce greater maximal (1RM) strength. With *sets* equated, very low-rep sets (2–4 reps) may yield somewhat less growth per set than moderate-rep sets. Evidence for this comes from a single small RCT.

### 2.2 Weekly volume
- **Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017** (J Sports Sci 35(11):1073–1082; DOI 10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197; verified). Meta-regression of 15 studies with a graded dose-response: each extra weekly set added about 0.37% growth. The authors suggested about 10 or more sets per muscle per week.
- **Baz-Valle et al. 2022** (J Hum Kinet 81:199–210; DOI 10.2478/hukin-2022-0017; verified). In trained men, 12–20 versus more than 20 weekly sets made no difference for the quadriceps or biceps. Higher volume helped the triceps. The authors proposed 12–20 sets per week as a sensible standard. The evidence base is small (6–7 studies).
- These results are consistent with Pelland 2026: more volume keeps helping, with shrinking returns.

### 2.3 Frequency
- **Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019** (J Sports Sci 37(11):1286–1295; DOI 10.1080/02640414.2018.1555906; verified). Meta-analysis of 25 studies. With volume equated, frequency had no meaningful effect on hypertrophy. Higher frequency mainly helps by making more total volume feasible. These findings agree with Pelland and ACSM.

### 2.4 Rest intervals
- **Singer et al. 2024** (Front Sports Act Living 6:1429789; DOI 10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789; verified). Bayesian meta-analysis of 9 studies. Resting more than about 60 s gave a small hypertrophy benefit. Beyond about 90 s there were no appreciable further differences, probably because the hit to volume load plateaus.
- **Schoenfeld et al. 2016** (J Strength Cond Res 30(7):1805–1812; DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000001272; verified). RCT in trained men: 3-minute rest beat 1-minute rest for strength and thigh growth.
- ACSM rated the rest-interval evidence as "cannot determine". A practical default is at least 90 s to 2 minutes, enough to keep reps and load up.

### 2.5 Range of motion and long muscle lengths
- **Pallarés et al. 2021** (Scand J Med Sci Sports 31(10):1866–1881; DOI 10.1111/sms.14006; verified). Meta-analysis of 16 studies. Full ROM beat partial ROM for strength (ES 0.56) and lower-limb hypertrophy (ES 0.88). Partials were not split by muscle length.
- **Wolf et al. 2023** (Int J Strength Cond 3(1); DOI 10.47206/ijsc.v3i1.182; verified via Crossref). Bayesian meta-analysis:
  - Overall, full ROM had only a trivial edge over partial ROM (SMD 0.12).
  - A subgroup analysis hinted that partials at **long** muscle lengths may beat full ROM for hypertrophy (SMD −0.28, interval crossing zero).
  - Strength gains were ROM-specific: training in a given range improved strength in that range.
- **Pedrosa et al. 2022** (Eur J Sport Sci 22(8):1250–1260; DOI 10.1080/17461391.2021.1927199; verified). In untrained women doing knee extensions, partials at long muscle lengths gave greater regional quadriceps growth than full ROM. Partials at short lengths did no better than control at distal sites.
- **Kassiano et al. 2023** (J Strength Cond Res 37(9):1746–1753; DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000004460; verified). In calf raises, partials at long lengths grew the medial gastrocnemius about 15%, versus about 7% for full ROM and 3% for short-length partials.
- **Maeo et al. 2021** (Med Sci Sports Exerc 53(4):825–837; DOI 10.1249/MSS.0000000000002523; verified). Seated leg curls (hamstrings lengthened) beat prone leg curls for hamstring growth (+14% vs +9%). The difference was driven by the biarticular hamstring muscles.
- **Maeo et al. 2023** (Eur J Sport Sci 23(7):1240–1250; DOI 10.1080/17461391.2022.2100279; verified). Overhead cable extensions gave about 1.4× the whole-triceps growth of neutral-arm extensions, despite lighter loads. Note: the user listed this study as "2021"; it was published in 2022 online and 2023 in print.
- **Summary:** Full ROM or a lengthened-biased ROM is the default. Partials at short muscle lengths appear inferior. Lengthened partials appear at least equivalent to full ROM, but the evidence is moderate, comes mostly from small studies on single muscles, and some subjects were untrained.

### 2.6 Progression
- **Plotkin et al. 2022** (PeerJ 10:e14142; DOI 10.7717/peerj.14142; verified). RCT in 43 trained lifters over 8 weeks. Adding reps at a fixed load and adding load at fixed reps gave similar growth. Load progression gave a slight, practically trivial strength edge. Both approaches are valid, which supports double progression.

### 2.7 Deloads
- **Coleman et al. 2024** (PeerJ 12:e16777; DOI 10.7717/peerj.16777; verified). RCT of 39 trained participants. A full week of training cessation midway through a 9-week program did not change hypertrophy but slightly reduced strength gains. This tested complete cessation, not a reduced-load week.
- **Bell et al. 2023** (Sports Med Open 9:87; DOI 10.1186/s40798-023-00633-0; verified). Delphi consensus of expert coaches; this is expert opinion, not trial evidence. Coaches agreed deloads can be either pre-planned or autoregulated. They reduce volume and/or proximity to failure, and their timing should follow the individual's response to training and their fatigue. The panel agreed on no fixed interval and no specific objective trigger markers.

### 2.8 Recovery time-course (brief)
- **Morán-Navarro et al. 2017** (Eur J Appl Physiol 117(12):2387–2399; DOI 10.1007/s00421-017-3725-7; verified). Sets to failure slowed neuromuscular and biochemical recovery by roughly 24–48 h compared with equal-volume non-failure sets. Non-failure sets largely recovered within 24–48 h.
- **Damas et al. 2016** (J Physiol 594(18):5209–5222; DOI 10.1113/JP272472; verified) and **Damas, Libardi & Ugrinowitsch 2018** (Eur J Appl Physiol 118(3):485–500; DOI 10.1007/s00421-017-3792-9; verified). Muscle damage is greatest in the first sessions of a new program and fades within about 3 weeks (the repeated-bout effect). Damage is not required for hypertrophy.
- **Summary:** Recovery time scales with volume, failure training and novelty. Trained lifters on familiar exercises usually recover within about 48–72 h.

### 2.9 Creatine (brief)
- **Burke et al. 2023** (Nutrients 15(9):2116; DOI 10.3390/nu15092116; verified). Meta-analysis of 10 RCTs using direct imaging. Creatine plus training gave a small extra hypertrophy effect (SMD 0.11), worth about 0.10–0.16 cm of muscle thickness. The user already takes creatine, so no change is needed.

### 2.10 Protein
- **Morton et al. 2018** (Br J Sports Med 52(6):376–384; DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608; verified). Meta-analysis of 49 RCTs and 1,863 participants:
  - Protein supplementation added about 0.3 kg of fat-free mass and about 2.5 kg of 1RM strength.
  - Gains in fat-free mass plateaued at a total intake of about 1.62 g/kg/day (95% CI 1.03–2.20). The upper end of that interval is a reasonable "safe" target.

  The user's whey simply helps reach this daily total.

### 2.11 Other verified supporting sources
- **Halperin et al. 2022** (Sports Med 52(2):377–390; DOI 10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x; verified). Lifters underestimated how many reps they had left by about 1 rep on average, with wide variation. Predictions were less accurate in high-rep sets (more than 12) and when made early in a set.
- **Sødal et al. 2023** (Sports Med Open 9:66; DOI 10.1186/s40798-023-00620-5; verified). Meta-analysis of 6 studies. Drop sets produced hypertrophy similar to traditional sets, often in less time.

## 3. Implications for the app's model

Status key: **Supported**, **Partly supported**, **Uncertain** (a reasonable heuristic without direct evidence) or **Contradicted**.

| Default | Status | Evidence and suggested change |
|---|---|---|
| **RIR credit:** 0–3 = 1.0, 4 = 0.6, 5 = 0.3, ≥6 = 0; unknown = 0.75 | Partly supported | **Evidence:**<br>• Refalo 2023, Refalo 2024 and ACSM support full credit at 0–3 RIR.<br>• Robinson 2024 shows growth declining non-linearly as RIR rises, but not reaching zero within the observed range.<br>• Self-reported RIR is biased low by about 1 rep (Halperin 2022).<br>**Change:** Consider a small non-zero credit at RIR 6–7 (for example 0.15) rather than a hard 0. For sets above 12 reps, treat logged RIR as about 1 rep higher than reported. Keep 0.75 for unknown RIR (Uncertain). |
| **Fractional sets:** direct 1.0, secondary 0.5, minor 0.25 | 1.0 and 0.5 supported; 0.25 uncertain | **Evidence:** Pelland 2026 found counting indirect sets at 0.5 fit best. No study tested a 0.25 tier.<br>**Change:** Keep 0.25 as a labeled heuristic. |
| **Weekly targets:** about 10–12 effective sets (min 6–8, max 16–18), diminishing returns | Supported, with a caveat | **Evidence:**<br>• ACSM recommends at least 10 sets per week.<br>• Pelland's tiers: about 4 sets is detectable, 5–10 is most efficient, and gains continue past 18 sets at lower efficiency.<br>**Caveat:** The "max" is a fatigue and efficiency bound, not a point where growth stops or reverses.<br>**Change:** Label the max as "diminishing returns", not "too much". The minimum could drop to about 4–6 sets. |
| **Very-low-rep sets** (<5 reps) ×0.8 | Uncertain, directionally supported | **Evidence:** Growth matches across loads when sets go to failure and volume is matched (Lopez 2021). With sets equated, 2–4RM sets gave less growth in one RCT (Schoenfeld 2016). |
| **Partial ROM** ×0.6 | Partly supported | **Evidence:** Short-length partials look clearly inferior (Pedrosa 2022, Kassiano 2023). The overall full-versus-partial difference is trivial (Wolf 2023).<br>**Change:** Apply ×0.6 only to partials at short muscle lengths, and consider about ×0.8 for unspecified partials. |
| **Lengthened partials** ×1.0 | Supported (moderate) | **Evidence:** Wolf subgroup, Pedrosa, Kassiano. Maeo 2021 and 2023 support favoring exercises that train muscles at long lengths. |
| **Drop-set segments** ×0.5 | Uncertain | **Evidence:** Drop sets give hypertrophy similar to traditional sets in less time (Sødal 2023). There is no per-segment evidence; ×0.5 is a conservative heuristic. |

Beyond the cap, taper credit (for example 0.5) rather than zeroing it. |
| **Double progression** (add load after all sets hit the top of the rep range at the intended RIR, two sessions in a row) | Supported | **Evidence:** Rep and load progression work similarly (Plotkin 2022); ACSM says progression matters long term. ### 3.1 Applied in the app (review of 2026-11-21)

- RIR 6–7 → 0.15; unspecified partial ROM ×0.8; lengthened partials ×1.0 (unchanged).
- Per-session ceiling: effective sets for one muscle above ~11 in a session are credited ×0.5 (taper, not zero). "Today ≈ N" is capped at ~60 % of the weekly target and ≤ 10 per session; the rest carries over.
- Lower end of the reference range for large muscles lowered from 8 to 6; upper bounds are labelled diminishing returns.
- Recovery windows recalibrated to 24/36/48/60/72 h by session size, extended by failure sets, novelty, soreness, sleep and fatigue; advisory only, and a session never blocks itself.
- Double progression: two consecutive top-of-range sessions, or one when every set reached the top with ≥ target RIR + 2 (clearly too light).
- Deload blocks halve targets (reduced volume, not full rest).
- **Not applied:** shifting reported RIR by +1 for sets above 12 reps (Halperin 2022). The average bias is ~1 rep but varies widely between people; a blanket shift would move every high-rep RIR-3 set from credit 1.0 to 0.6. Reported RIR is used as-is.

**Overall:** The model is broadly consistent with current evidence. The best-supported changes are:
1. Give the high-RIR tail a small non-zero credit.
2. Raise the per-session cap to about 10–11 fractional sets, with tapering credit rather than a hard cutoff.
3. Distinguish short-length from unspecified partials.
4. Present weekly maximums as efficiency limits, not harm thresholds.
